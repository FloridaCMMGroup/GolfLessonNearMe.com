Golf lessons near me: how to find a golf instructor worth your money

The closest golf coach isn't always the right one. Here's how to tell the difference before you spend a dollar, and what to do if there's no great coach near you.

Why I'm writing this

Almost every week, a new client tells me the same story. They searched "golf lessons near me," booked whoever was closest, took three lessons, and walked away hitting it worse than when they started. They spent the money. They spent the time. And they almost quit the game.

That's not a golfer problem. It's a choosing-a-coach problem. I first wrote about how to choose a golf instructor for PGA.com back in 2003, and I updated it for PGA.com in October 2019. The game has changed since then, and so has coaching. But after 30+ years of coaching, and hiring and training more than 55 coaches as National Director of Instruction for Golf Digest Schools, the fundamentals of choosing the right coach haven't changed at all. Here they are, updated for today.

Start with compatibility

Before credentials, before price, before location, make sure the two of you fit. Your personalities, your goals, what you each believe about how the game should be played, and whether the coach can relate to what you need. The best coach in the world won't help you if you dread every session.

Do your research

Ask your friends and playing partners who they've worked with. Ask about price, reputation, location, and most of all, whether they actually got better. Then call the coach, or meet in person, before you book anything. A good coach is happy to talk about your game and get to know you first. If they won't take ten minutes on the phone, that tells you something.

What credentials actually matter

PGA or LPGA membership
Plenty of people call themselves golf coaches. Some other organizations that train instructors have raised their standards over the years, and that's good for golfers. But you won't find better golf instruction than from a PGA or LPGA member. Every one of them has completed years of training and has to keep updating it.
A coach who actually coaches
Many golf professionals spend most of their week running a shop, tournaments and member events. Ask how much of their week is spent teaching golfers like you.
Teaching recognition
Section awards, magazine rankings and advanced certifications mean other professionals have looked at their work. They don't tell you everything, but they tell you something.
Results with golfers like you
A coach who works mostly with elite juniors may not be the right fit for a weekend golfer who plays twice a month. Ask who they usually coach.

Video, swing aids and follow-up

Video should show you how to improve
Every session should use video, and you should be able to take it with you. If your coach can't show you your swing and explain what needs to change, you're behind the times.
A swing aid has one job
It should help you feel the difference between what you're doing now and what your coach wants you to feel. If it doesn't give you that feedback, it's the wrong aid for you, no matter what it cost.
Follow-up isn't optional
You should be able to reach your coach between sessions, in person or online, about your practice, your schedule and your progress. A coach who disappears until your next paid lesson isn't coaching you.

Look for a coach with a team. The game has gotten too big for one person to know everything. The best coaches surround themselves with experts: fitness trainers, sports psychologists, nutritionists and other coaches. You may never need all of them. But having them available when you do can make a huge difference.

Five questions to ask before you book golf lessons near you

  1. What will we work on first, and why? A good coach starts by finding out where your strokes go, not by rebuilding your swing on day one.
  2. How will we measure progress? Look for an answer with numbers in it: scores, putts, penalty strokes or launch monitor data.
  3. What happens between lessons? You should leave every session with a clear practice plan, not just a feeling.
  4. How many sessions do most golfers need to see a change? An honest coach gives you a range and explains why.
  5. Do you coach on the golf course? The range can't show how you make decisions. The course can.

Red flags

A full swing overhaul in your first lesson
Big changes can be right, but almost never before the coach knows your game.
No plan between sessions
If you leave with nothing to practice, you paid for a range session with company.
Every golfer swings the same way
A good coach works with your body, your schedule and your goals. Not a template.

No interest in your short game. If the whole lesson is full swing, half of your score is being ignored. It is!

The checklist

Before you book golf lessons near you, answer these. If you can't answer yes to at least six, keep looking.

  1. Do your personalities and goals fit?
  2. Did a friend or playing partner recommend them, and did that golfer actually improve?
  3. Is the coach a PGA or LPGA member, and how much of their week is coaching?
  4. Did they talk with you about your goals, your typical miss and how often you play before you booked?
  5. Can they explain how they'll measure your progress?
  6. Do they use video you can take with you?
  7. Can you reach them between sessions?
  8. Will you leave each session with a practice plan?
